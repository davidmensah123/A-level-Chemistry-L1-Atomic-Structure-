01 Historical Evolution of Atomic Models

The atomic model was iteratively refined over centuries through rigorous experimental evidence. Click each scientist below to inspect the historical model, experimental foundation, and key limitations:

Billiard Ball Model 1803

John Dalton: Solid Indivisible Sphere

Dalton proposed that all matter is composed of tiny, solid, indivisible spheres termed atoms. He stated that atoms of a given element are identical in mass and properties, and chemical reactions involve the rearrangement of these indivisible units.

Experimental Evidence & Limitation:

Evidence: Law of conservation of mass and constant chemical composition ratios.
Limitation: Could not explain the existence of subatomic charges or the emission of cathode rays discovered later by Thomson.

02 The Size Scale of an Atom vs. Nucleus

An atom consists of a central, positively charged nucleus surrounded by electron clouds. Most of the atom is completely empty space:

Atomic Diameter
\(\sim 1 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}\)

\(1\text{ Ångström } (1\text{ Å}) = 0.1\text{ nm}\). Determined by the outermost boundary of the electron clouds.

Nuclear Diameter
\(\sim 1 \times 10^{-15}\text{ m}\)

\(1\text{ femtometre } (1\text{ fm})\). Contains virtually the entirety (\(>99.95\%\)) of the atomic mass.

Relative Scale Ratio
\(100,000 \times\) Larger

\[\frac{10^{-10}\text{ m}}{10^{-15}\text{ m}} = 10^5\] If the nucleus were the size of a marble in the center of a football stadium, the outer electrons would orbit at the outer perimeter seats.

How Does the Nucleus Hold Together? (Strong Nuclear Force)

Protons are all positively charged. According to Coulomb’s Law, like charges repel with a repulsive electrostatic force: \[F_e = \frac{k_e q_1 q_2}{r^2}\] At nuclear distances (\(\sim 10^{-15}\text{ m}\)), this electrostatic repulsion is enormous (\(>200\text{ N}\) between two adjacent protons).

The Strong Nuclear Force (SNF)

An attractive fundamental force acting between all nucleons (proton-proton, neutron-neutron, and proton-neutron). It is roughly \(100\times\) stronger than electrostatic repulsion, holding the nucleus together.

Short-Range Limitation

The strong nuclear force only acts over very short distances (\(\sim 1\text{ to }3\text{ fm}\)). Beyond \(3\text{ fm}\), it drops to zero. Hence, as nuclei grow larger, additional neutrons are required to dilute proton-proton repulsion.

04 Atomic Number (Z), Mass Number (A), & Isotopes

A nuclide is represented using standard \({}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\) notation:

\[{}^{A}_{Z}\text{X}\]

• Mass Number (\(A\)): Total number of nucleons (protons + neutrons) in the nucleus.

• Atomic Number (\(Z\)): Number of protons in the nucleus. Identifies the chemical element.

• Number of Neutrons (\(N\)): \[N = A - Z\]

What are Isotopes?

"Isotopes are atoms of the same element with the same number of protons (same atomic number \(Z\)), but different numbers of neutrons (different mass number \(A\))."
Why do isotopes react in the exact same chemical manner?
Chemical reactions involve the sharing, loss, or gain of valence electrons. Because different isotopes of an element have the exact same number and spatial arrangement of electrons, their chemical properties are identical. They differ only in physical properties that depend on mass (e.g. density, rate of diffusion, boiling points).

05 Ion Formation: Cations & Anions

An ion is an atom or group of bonded atoms that has acquired an electrical charge due to the loss or gain of valence electrons:

Cation (Positive Ion)

Formed when an atom loses electrons. Cations have more protons than electrons, resulting in an overall positive charge. Metals in Groups 1, 2, and 3 typically form cations (e.g., \(\text{Na}^+\), \(\text{Mg}^{2+}\), \(\text{Al}^{3+}\)).

\[\text{Overall Charge} = (\text{Number of Protons}) - (\text{Number of Electrons})\]
Anion (Negative Ion)

Formed when an atom gains electrons. Anions have more electrons than protons, resulting in an overall negative charge. Non-metals in Groups 5, 6, and 7 typically form anions (e.g., \(\text{N}^{3-}\), \(\text{O}^{2-}\), \(\text{Cl}^-\)).

Example: \(\text{O}^{2-}\) has 8 protons and 10 electrons: \(8 - 10 = -2\).
